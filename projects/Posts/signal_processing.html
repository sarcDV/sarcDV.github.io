Fourier Analysis

Decomposing Signals into Frequency Components

The Fourier Transform is a fundamental tool in signal processing that decomposes a function into its constituent frequencies. This mathematical operation transforms a signal from its original domain (often time or space) to a representation in the frequency domain.

In physics, this corresponds to analyzing how much of each frequency component is present in a wave. The Fourier Transform has applications across many fields including acoustics, optics, image processing, and quantum mechanics.

The mathematical definition of the continuous Fourier Transform is:

\[F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-i\omega t} dt\]

Where \(f(t)\) is the time-domain signal and \(F(\omega)\) is its frequency-domain representation.

Removing Unwanted Frequency Components

Filters are systems that remove or attenuate certain frequency components from a signal while preserving others. This process is essential in many applications such as audio processing, communications, and image enhancement.

There are four main types of filters:

  • Low-pass filters allow low frequencies to pass while attenuating high frequencies
  • High-pass filters allow high frequencies to pass while attenuating low frequencies
  • Band-pass filters allow a specific range of frequencies to pass
  • Band-stop filters attenuate a specific range of frequencies

Filters can be implemented using various technologies including electronic circuits, digital algorithms, and optical systems. The behavior of a filter is characterized by its transfer function, which describes how it responds to different frequencies.

Signal Modulation

Encoding Information in Carrier Waves

Modulation is the process of varying one or more properties of a periodic waveform, called the carrier signal, with a separate signal that contains the information to be transmitted.

Key modulation techniques include:

  • Amplitude Modulation (AM): The amplitude of the carrier wave is varied in proportion to the message signal
  • Frequency Modulation (FM): The frequency of the carrier wave is varied in proportion to the message signal
  • Phase Modulation (PM): The phase of the carrier wave is varied in proportion to the message signal

Modulation enables the transmission of information over long distances and allows multiple signals to share the same transmission medium through frequency division multiplexing. This principle is fundamental to radio, television, and cellular communications.

Converting Continuous Signals to Discrete

The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem states that a continuous signal can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples if the sampling frequency is at least twice the highest frequency present in the signal.

This critical frequency is known as the Nyquist rate:

\[f_s \geq 2f_{max}\]

Where \(f_s\) is the sampling frequency and \(f_{max}\) is the highest frequency in the signal.

If this condition is not met, aliasing occurs, where higher frequencies appear as lower frequencies in the sampled signal. To prevent aliasing, anti-aliasing filters are used before sampling to remove frequency components above the Nyquist frequency.

The reconstruction of the original signal from its samples is performed using interpolation, with the ideal reconstruction filter being the sinc function:

\[x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] \cdot \text{sinc}\left(\frac{t - nT}{T}\right)\]
