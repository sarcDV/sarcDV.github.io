Collective Dynamics of “Small-World” Networks

Watts & Strogatz, Nature 393, 440–442 (1998)

Exploring the balance of clustering and path length in real-world networks

Original paper: https://www.nature.com/articles/30918

Introduction

Numerous self-organising systems, including biological oscillators, excitable media, neural networks, spatial games, genetic control networks, and Josephson junction arrays, have been modelled using networks of coupled dynamical systems. Typically, it is assumed that the connection topology is either entirely random or completely regular. However, a lot of social, technological, and biological networks fall somewhere in the middle.

Here, we examine basic network models that can be adjusted to fall anywhere in the middle: conventional networks that have been "rewired" to add more and more disorder. We discover that these systems can have tiny characteristic path lengths, similar to random graphs, but can also be strongly clustered, similar to regular lattices. By similarity with the small-world phenomena (often referred to as six degrees of separation), we refer to these networks as "small-worlds."

It is demonstrated that the electricity grid in the western United States, the neural network of the worm Caenorhabditis elegans, and the collaboration graph of movie stars are examples of small-world networks. Small-world coupling dynamical system models exhibit improved synchronizability, computing capacity, and signal propagation speed. Specifically, compared to normal lattices, infectious diseases propagate more readily in small-world networks.

Many real-world networks—social, biological, technological—exhibit:

  • High clustering: Your friends are likely friends with each other.
  • Short path lengths: You can reach anyone in the network in just a few steps (“six degrees of separation”).

Regular lattices have high clustering but long paths; random graphs have short paths but low clustering. Watts and Strogatz introduced a model that bridges this gap.

Mathematical Formulation

We examine the subsequent random rewiring process to interpolate between regular and random networks. We begin by rewiring every edge randomly with probability \( p \) on a ring lattice consisting of \( n \) nodes and \( k \) edges per vertex. We may "tune" the graph between regularity (\( p = 0 \)) and disorder (\( p = 1 \)) using this approach, which enables us to examine the little understood transitional area \( 0 < p < 1 \).

1. Ring Lattice Initialization

Start with \( n \) nodes in a ring. Each node connects to its \( k/2 \) nearest neighbors on each side (so \( k \) must be even).

2. Rewiring with Probability \( p \)

For each node \( i \), consider each edge to a clockwise neighbor \( j \). With probability \( p \in [0,1] \), rewire that edge to a randomly chosen node \( l \neq i \), avoiding self-loops and duplicate edges.

3. Key Metrics

The structure is characterized by two quantities:

  • Average path length, a global characteristic, quantifies the average distance between two graph nodes: \[ L(p) = \frac{1}{n(n-1)} \sum_{i \neq j} d_{ij} \] where \( d_{ij} \) is the shortest path between \( i \) and \( j \).
  • Clustering coefficient, a local characteristic, quantifies how connected a node's neighbors are to each other: \[ C(p) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{2 e_i}{k_i(k_i - 1)} \] where \( e_i \) is the number of edges between neighbors of node \( i \), and \( k_i \) is its degree.

4. Small-World Regime

The networks of interest to us have many nodes with sparse connections, but not so sparse that the graph is in danger of becoming disconnected.

As \( p \) increases from 0 to 1:

  • At \( p = 0 \) (regular lattice): High clustering \( C(0) \) and long path length \( L(0) \).
  • At \( p = 1 \) (random graph): Low clustering \( C(1) \approx k/n \) and short path length \( L(1) \approx \ln(n)/\ln(k) \).

For small \( p \) (e.g., \( p \approx 0.01 \)), \( L(p) \approx L_{\text{random}} \) while \( C(p) \gg C_{\text{random}} \). This defines the small-world regime.
